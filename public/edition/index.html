What changed

President Donald Trump said envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner held a three-hour meeting with Iranian mediators on Tuesday and called it productive, but he published no terms. The contact followed his U.N. threat to annihilate Iran if no deal is reached. At the same gathering, Qatar’s Amir said reopening the Strait of Hormuz and returning to negotiations could end the crisis, but argued that the deeper regional failure is the absence of a collective-security system respecting sovereignty and non-interference.

Why it matters

Direct contact is more consequential than another prediction that a deal is close, yet the public evidence still shows process rather than settlement. Hormuz, attacks on Gulf infrastructure and the war’s energy shock cannot be stabilized by a private meeting alone. Qatar’s proposal shifts the frame from a bilateral U.S.–Iran bargain toward rules that neighboring states would have to accept and enforce. That is a larger diplomatic project—and a harder one.

A second layer covers the U.S.–Iran dispute itself: military pressure, sanctions, security guarantees and whatever verification a deal requires. Qatar’s Amir added a third layer—regional rules intended to prevent one confrontation from pulling neighboring states and their infrastructure into war. What changed

The dollar index stood around 100.56 in early Wednesday trading, near its strongest level in two months. Markets expect more tightening from major central banks after recent hikes and hawkish guidance. Brent traded near $99.22 a barrel, down from recent highs on hopes that U.N. diplomacy could advance an Iran settlement, but still 37% above its level when the conflict began in late February.

The Iran war pushed up energy prices and inflation expectations, encouraging central banks to tighten. Now crude can fall on negotiation hopes while interest-rate expectations remain elevated because domestic demand and broader inflation are still firm. Currency values respond to expected returns across economies, not only commodity prices. The present dollar level is therefore evidence of a rate story as well as a geopolitical one.

Food-system risk

The next inflation shock may come from weather layered onto war.

The forecast is probabilistic, but physical effects are already visible.
05 · Climate, food & Asian economiesAP · Tuesday

A potentially record El Niño is turning Asia’s drought into a second global food shock.

What changed

The U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration assigns a 69% chance that the current El Niño will become the strongest in records dating to 1950. Drought, heat, fires and depleted water supplies are already affecting parts of Southeast Asia. The World Food Program estimates the event could push at least 49 million additional people into acute food insecurity by the end of 2027.

Why it matters

Energy and fertilizer stress from the Iran war has already reduced governments’ fiscal room and raised farm costs. A severe weather shock can now hit rice, palm oil, fisheries and other supply chains at the same time. The risk is not merely a bad harvest in one country; export restrictions and precautionary buying can transmit local shortages into world prices.

Watch next

The expected December peak; rainfall and reservoir data; rice and palm-oil yields; wildfire and haze conditions; export controls; food-subsidy spending; WFP funding; and whether the 69% forecast is revised as Pacific temperatures evolve.

A climate event becomes an economic cascade

El Niño begins with unusually warm Pacific waters and alters rainfall across distant regions. The agricultural damage depends on timing, local crops, irrigation and policy responses. A moderate harvest loss can become a larger price shock when countries restrict exports or importers build inventories simultaneously. Current energy and fertilizer constraints make adaptation costlier and leave less public money for subsidies or emergency imports.

08 · Aviation systems & resilienceAP · Tuesday

The Northeast aviation outage became a case for a $20 billion U.S. control-system overhaul.

What changed

Airlines were still repositioning aircraft and crews on Tuesday after Monday’s communications failure, with nearly 100 flights canceled by midday. AP reported that a switch on the primary line at the Philadelphia control facility failed; the backup fiber had separately been cut by railway construction. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy used the outage to press Congress for another $20 billion for the next modernization phase.

Why it matters

The incident moved from a generic technical failure to a specific resilience lesson: the primary system and its supposed backup were both unavailable. Restoration did not immediately restore the network because tightly scheduled fleets and crews had moved out of place. Funding may be justified, but the design test is whether new communications paths are genuinely independent and routinely exercised.

Network recovery lasts longer than repair

An air-traffic communications fault can stop arrivals immediately. Once flights are canceled or diverted, crews time out, aircraft miss later rotations and gates and passenger connections fall out of sequence. That is why Tuesday still carried cancellations after Monday evening’s technical restoration. The infrastructure question is equally practical: redundancy must avoid common routes and common dependencies, or a second line exists on paper without providing real resilience.

JAPAN, RICE & MARKET INFRASTRUCTURE
1600s–PRESENT

09

Osaka’s rice merchants built a futures market before finance had the language for one.

In Tokugawa Japan, tax rice flowed into Osaka warehouses while samurai governments needed spendable money before every harvest or shipment arrived. Merchants turned warehouse claims and future delivery into tradable contracts. In 1730, the shogunate authorized the Dōjima market, complete with membership, margining, cash settlement and clearing functions. The result was not merely speculation: it was an information and risk system built around the country’s fiscal grain.

Why remember this?

Futures markets emerged at Dōjima because a physical supply chain, a credit system and a volatile fiscal asset all needed one trusted price—and rules for surviving disagreement about the future.
1 · Rice was food, tax revenue and political finance

Tokugawa Japan’s domains collected much of their revenue in rice. Bales moved from producing regions to warehouse-residences in commercial cities, especially Osaka. The ruling warrior class, however, paid expenses in money. That created a recurring conversion problem: the value of the tax base changed with harvests, transport and demand, while obligations continued every month. Osaka’s warehouse district became the place where physical rice, official finance and merchant credit met. The market did not begin with an abstract desire to invent derivatives. It began because people held claims on a crop whose arrival, quality and price were uncertain, and they needed liquidity before every bale could be inspected and sold.

2 · Warehouse claims became easier to move than grain

Domains auctioned stored tax rice and issued rice bills promising exchange for specified quantities. Some bills covered rice already in Osaka; others referred to deliveries still on the way or future harvests. The paper claim could circulate without the holder moving heavy bales through the city. That separation of ownership from immediate physical possession made trade faster, but it also required trust in issuers, warehouses and settlement. A rice bill was useful only if participants believed that the underlying obligation would be honored. The financial instrument therefore grew out of logistics. Warehousing created standardized claims; standardized claims created a market; and the market created demand for common rules.

3 · Dōjima organized disagreement about tomorrow’s price

By the late seventeenth century, rice trading had concentrated around Dōjima. In 1730, the Tokugawa shogunate formally authorized both a spot market for rice bills and a book-based futures market in representative grades. Traders could agree today on a price for settlement later, allowing someone exposed to falling rice prices to transfer part of that risk to someone willing to take the opposite view. The Bank of Japan describes the market as having margining and cash settlement; Japan Exchange Group notes membership and clearing functions resembling later exchanges. Those details matter. A future price becomes socially useful only when participants know who may trade, how losses are covered and how contracts end.

4 · Price discovery traveled faster than the commodity

The Dōjima price became a reference beyond the streets where trading occurred. Couriers and flag signals carried quotations hundreds of kilometers to Edo and other cities. Rice itself moved by ship and cart; information about rice moved through a communications network. That difference in speed let a local market coordinate decisions across regions. A domain assessing revenue, a merchant financing inventory and a buyer planning future needs could respond to a shared signal rather than isolated local rumors. The exchange’s importance therefore came from more than the volume traded. It produced a public benchmark from private bids, compressing dispersed judgments about harvests, transport, inventories and demand into one observable price.

5 · Hedging and speculation were inseparable partners

A producer or holder of rice could use futures to reduce exposure to a price fall. That contract required another participant willing to accept the risk, perhaps because that trader expected prices to rise. Modern debates often imagine hedging as legitimate and speculation as an unwanted addition, but a market needs counterparties and liquidity. Historical research also cautions against romanticizing Dōjima as perfectly efficient. Studies find changing performance across seasons and periods, with producer hedging stronger in some markets and financial trading more prominent in others. The useful achievement was not eliminating volatility or bad judgment. It was creating a governed arena in which different expectations could meet and obligations could be settled.

6 · Government alternated between using the market and blaming it

The shogunate authorized Dōjima partly because rice prices were politically important. Later governments also intervened when prices became unacceptable. Japan Exchange Group records that the Meiji government banned Dōjima trading in 1869 amid steep price rises, then revived a rice exchange in 1871 to restore active trade. This cycle is familiar: authorities value market liquidity and information during normal times, then suspect speculation when prices cause hardship. Sometimes manipulation is real; sometimes the market is revealing scarcity that policy would rather not confront. Closing an exchange can suppress a signal without producing more grain. The institutional challenge is to police abuse while preserving price discovery and risk transfer.

7 · A futures market is infrastructure, not a prediction machine

Dōjima’s lasting lesson is not that traders learned to see the future. Futures prices can be wrong, distorted or overwhelmed by events. The achievement was to build a system around uncertainty: standardized claims, membership, collateral, clearing, settlement and rapid communication. Each element answered a practical failure mode. Without standards, contracts were incomparable. Without margin, losing positions could default. Without clearing and enforcement, a chain of private promises could collapse. Without price transmission, the benchmark stayed local. Modern exchanges use electronic order books and global clearing houses, but the underlying problem is recognizable. Markets become durable when their rules make disagreement survivable.

8 · The market’s origin changes how finance should be judged

The world’s early organized futures market grew around a staple food and a tax system, not around financial fashion. It helped turn future rice into present liquidity and scattered information into a common price. It also created opportunities for leverage, speculation and political conflict. Both sides belong in the history. A derivative is neither productive nor destructive by name; its value depends on the exposure it serves, the transparency of its price, the integrity of settlement and the concentration of losses when forecasts fail. Dōjima’s merchants did not solve uncertainty. They built civic machinery for carrying it.
