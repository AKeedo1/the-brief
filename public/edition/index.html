What changed

Advance data showed third-quarter GDP rising 3.4%, with private domestic demand up 4.9%; annual PCE inflation increased from 3.4% to 3.6% in September and inflation-adjusted consumer spending was unchanged that month.

Why it matters

The economy entered the fourth quarter with more momentum than expected, powered by services and equipment investment, yet the final month shows higher prices absorbing nominal spending. That combination strengthens the case for another Federal Reserve increase rather than relief for borrowers.

Watch next

AI investment and services carried the quarter while September exposed the pressure underneath

The Bureau of Economic Analysis estimates that real GDP rose at a 3.4% annual rate in the third quarter, up from 2.2% in the second. Consumer spending increased at a 3.8% rate and business equipment investment surged 20.9%, with information-processing equipment making the largest contribution. Final sales to private domestic purchasers—a cleaner measure of underlying private demand—grew 4.9%. Yet the September detail was less comfortable. The PCE price index rose 0.5% in the month and 3.6% from a year earlier; core PCE rose 3.1% over twelve months. Nominal consumer spending increased 0.5%, but after inflation it was flat, and the saving rate slipped to 3.8%. This is not a contradiction so much as a change in the composition of growth. Strong third-quarter services and AI-related investment lifted the aggregate, while higher energy and goods prices narrowed household purchasing power at quarter-end. The advance estimate establishes momentum; it does not guarantee the fourth quarter.

Technology & consumer

The cloud engine is accelerating; the cash engine is being rebuilt around it.

Amazon shows why strong operating profit and negative free cash flow can coexist during an infrastructure cycle.
03 · AI infrastructure, retail & capital allocationAmazon · Thursday close

Amazon posted its fastest AWS growth in nearly five years—and a $42.4 billion trailing free-cash-flow outflow.

What changed

AWS revenue rose 39.3% to $46.0 billion and operating income reached $17.8 billion, while Amazon's total operating margin hit a record 13.9%; AI-led property and equipment spending turned trailing free cash flow from a $14.8 billion inflow to a $42.4 billion outflow.

Why it matters

This is unusually clear evidence of both AI demand and its financing burden inside the same company. Amazon's operations are generating more cash, but infrastructure purchases are consuming it faster.

Watch next

The conversion of AWS backlog into revenue, 2026 cash capital expenditure of about $220 billion, fourth-quarter retail demand and whether 2027–28 cloud growth meets management's forecast. Results are reported; future growth and returns are company guidance.

A profitable quarter does not remove the balance-sheet test

Amazon's third-quarter net sales rose 12% to $201.9 billion and operating income increased 61% to $28.1 billion, producing the company's highest operating margin at 13.9%. AWS was the centre of gravity: sales reached $46.0 billion, up 39.3%, and segment operating income rose to $17.8 billion. The cash-flow statement tells a different but compatible story. Trailing operating cash flow increased 29% to $168.3 billion, yet net property and equipment purchases reached $210.7 billion. Amazon therefore reported a $42.4 billion trailing free-cash-flow outflow, versus a $14.8 billion inflow a year earlier, and attributed the increase mainly to artificial-intelligence investment. Management still expects roughly $220 billion of cash capital expenditure in 2026 and projects AWS growth above 40% in both 2027 and 2028. Those forecasts may prove right; they are not results. The investor question is how quickly this physical build turns into durable cash after depreciation, energy and financing costs.

Curiosity Corner

A spider can enter two distinct stages of regulated sleep—including one that looks strikingly like REM.

University of Bonn researchers filmed the Australian jumping spider Portia fimbriata over several days and used acoustic stimuli and sleep disruption to test whether rest was regulated. They found two stages, including a REM-like phase with retinal movements and muscle twitches. The study provides behavioural evidence, not proof that spiders dream; no brain activity was measured. Its value is evolutionary: a tiny, very different nervous system may help reveal which functions of sleep are ancient and universal.

The Long View · Daily history

6 min

United States & the Moon · 1961–1972

LV

Apollo's guidance computer did not survive the Moon landing by being powerful. It survived by knowing what mattered first.

Minutes before Apollo 11 touched down, the lunar module's computer began reporting 1201 and 1202 alarms. The machine had less working memory than a modern email attachment and was being asked to fly, navigate, display and respond at once. Its decisive feature was not raw capacity but a priority system built to discard lower-value work under overload.

Why remember this?

Reliable systems do not assume overload will never happen. They define the work that must survive it, make failure visible and preserve enough context to restart safely. Apollo's computer succeeded because hardware, software, simulation and human judgement formed one recovery system.
1 · The Moon forced guidance into the vehicle

When NASA awarded MIT's Instrumentation Laboratory the Apollo guidance contract in 1961, a signal to the Moon and back took too long for Earth to fly every second of the landing. The computer had to travel with the crew. It also had to survive launch vibration, radiation, scarce electrical power and severe limits on weight. NASA chose MIT partly because its Polaris missile work had already forced navigation into constrained hardware. Even so, requirements remained unsettled until late 1962, and engineers later said that a complete early specification might have made the problem look impossible. Autonomy began not as a product feature but as a physical consequence of distance. The machine needed to turn navigation measurements into control decisions when Houston could advise but could not steer in real time.

2 · Scarcity made priorities part of the architecture

The Apollo Guidance Computer packed roughly 2,000 sixteen-bit words of erasable memory and 36,000 words of fixed core-rope memory into about one cubic foot. Fixed programs were physically encoded by threading wires through or around magnetic cores; late software changes therefore had manufacturing consequences. With so little working memory and processing time, the executive could not treat every task equally. It scheduled work by priority and linked jobs to events. Guidance and engine control belonged above display updates or calculations that could wait. This was more than making code small. The designers embedded a theory of mission value inside the operating system: when capacity became scarce, the machine needed a principled answer to which work would continue. The architecture converted scarcity from an accident into a condition the software could manage.

3 · Testing created the confidence to continue through an alarm

Priority scheduling alone could not tell Mission Control whether a particular alarm was safe. NASA and MIT built high-fidelity digital and hardware-in-the-loop simulations, exercised flight programs and catalogued computer responses. During training, guidance officer Steve Bales and computer specialist Jack Garman had confronted overload alarms and developed rules for when the landing could continue. Garman kept a handwritten list of alarm codes at his console. This mattered because the four-digit display did not explain itself to the astronauts. It compressed an internal condition into a code that only a trained organisation could interpret. The alarm was therefore a handoff between layers: the computer detected overload, the code exposed it, specialists interpreted it and the flight director retained authority to abort. Reliability existed in the relationship among them.

4 · On 20 July 1969, the computer became overloaded for a reason nobody understood in real time

As Eagle descended, Buzz Aldrin called a 1202 alarm. More 1201 and 1202 alarms followed. The computer's executive queues were overloaded, in part because the rendezvous radar switch caused the system to keep seeking data it did not need for landing. Nobody in the room immediately knew that full causal chain. What they could see was more important: after each restart, the computer resumed the highest-priority guidance work and continued producing credible navigation. Garman advised that the mission could proceed; Bales passed the go recommendation; flight director Gene Kranz accepted it. The decision was not blind faith in software. It was a bounded inference: essential tasks were still executing, the alarm matched a known class and the spacecraft remained within expected behaviour. Apollo 11 landed with a computer that had shed work without surrendering control.

5 · The restart was selective, not amnesia

A conventional crash-and-reboot story misses the clever part. The Apollo software used restart protection to preserve the state of critical programs and re-establish essential jobs after an overload. Lower-priority tasks that had contributed to congestion were not all resumed. The system could therefore recover while the vehicle was still moving. That property turned an overload from a total discontinuity into controlled degradation. It also explains why the alarms were evidence of the design working, even though the trigger exposed a systems-integration flaw involving switch settings, radar inputs and assumptions across teams. NASA investigated immediately after landing because the lunar module had to launch again. Telemetry and simulation revealed the radar-related cycle stealing. The lesson was not that testing had failed completely. It was that complex systems can meet unanticipated combinations only if their recovery behaviour is designed before the combination occurs.

6 · Apollo's durable lesson is to engineer what survives overload

Apollo's computer never suffered a hardware failure in flight, but its historical importance is broader than perfect hardware. The programme joined strict production quality, priority-driven software, simulation, alarms and disciplined human authority into one system. Modern software often treats overload as a capacity-planning problem: add processors, memory or servers. Apollo's deeper answer was to decide in advance which functions could degrade and which could not. That principle now appears in fault-tolerant aircraft, medical devices, payments and online services, though the details differ. Redundancy without priority can preserve too much work; alarms without trained interpretation can merely export confusion. Reliable systems do not promise that nothing will go wrong. They preserve the work that matters, expose what changed and give people enough evidence to make the next decision.
