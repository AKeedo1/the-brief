Technology & business

AI governance is moving through state courtrooms as well as regulators.

The filings are confirmed; every claim about harm, deception and responsibility remains to be proved.
02 · AI products, safety & lawAP · Thursday

At least eight U.S. states sued OpenAI, turning consumer-protection law into a new front in AI governance.

What changed

State attorneys general accused OpenAI of harming children, deceiving adults and releasing systems without adequate safeguards. The complaints also allege that some models enabled cyber abuse. These are legal allegations; courts have not tested them and OpenAI's liability has not been established.

Why it matters

The cases could shape product warnings, age protections, model-release practices and evidence disclosure before federal lawmakers agree on a comprehensive AI regime. For the industry, compliance may fragment state by state.

Watch next

OpenAI's formal responses, requests for injunctions, whether the cases are consolidated, discovery about internal safety decisions and the boundary courts draw between a general-purpose tool and a consumer product making actionable representations.

Why state consumer law matters

State attorneys general do not need to wait for a new federal AI statute to act. Existing rules against deceptive or unfair business practices can reach marketing claims, safety disclosures and the design of products used by minors. That route is slower and messier than one national standard, but it can force document disclosure and concrete remedies.

You already know

At least eight states filed cases under consumer-protection theories and described risks to children and other users.

New now

The coordinated litigation moves the dispute from voluntary safeguards and policy debate into adversarial proceedings with discovery and judicial review.

Confirmed

Multiple state attorneys general filed lawsuits identifying alleged product, marketing and safety failures.

Not established

The allegations have not been adjudicated, and the eventual remedies or effect on product releases remain unknown.

Curiosity Corner

An anaesthetised hippocampus can still sort words, detect grammar and anticipate meaning.

In seven epilepsy-surgery patients, researchers recorded individual hippocampal neurons under propofol anaesthesia. Four heard spoken stories; their neural signals retained information about semantic categories and parts of speech, and even carried predictive information about upcoming words. This does not show conscious understanding or memory: it shows that sophisticated local processing can survive while large-scale conscious access is suppressed.

The Long View · Daily history

6 min

1842–1930s · United States, Britain and global medicine

049

Ether Day did not discover painless surgery. It made the idea public, credible and portable.

On 16 October 1846, a packed operating theatre saw pain separated from the surgeon's knife—and medicine acquired a new profession, new risks and a different frontier of possibility.

Why remember this?

Modern anaesthesia emerged from more than a chemical. Ether was already known, and earlier practitioners had used it. The breakthrough was a convincing public demonstration before a trusted medical audience, followed by rapid publication, replication and the slow construction of specialist practice around keeping an unconscious patient alive.
1 · Before anaesthesia, speed was mercy

Surgery before the 1840s was constrained by conscious pain. Skilled surgeons prized speed because every extra second meant more suffering, movement and shock. Alcohol, opium, compression and other methods could dull pain, but none made prolonged, controlled unconsciousness reliable enough to transform the operating room. The absence of anaesthesia limited not just compassion but the kinds of operations surgeons could attempt.

2 · The chemical was not the secret

Diethyl ether had been made and inhaled long before 1846, sometimes as entertainment. In 1842, Georgia physician Crawford Long used ether while removing neck cysts, then repeated the method but did not publish until 1849. Dentist Horace Wells tried to demonstrate nitrous oxide at Massachusetts General Hospital in 1845; the patient cried out and the audience dismissed the attempt. Knowledge existed, but it had not yet won institutional trust.

3 · A theatre became a proof machine

On 16 October 1846, dentist William T. G. Morton brought an ether inhaler into Massachusetts General Hospital's surgical amphitheatre. Surgeon John Collins Warren removed a tumour from Edward Gilbert Abbott's neck while Abbott remained insensible to the operation. The audience mattered as much as the device: respected witnesses could judge the result, repeat it and carry the claim beyond a private room.

4 · Publication turned spectacle into practice

Harvard surgeon Henry Jacob Bigelow reported the result in the Boston Medical and Surgical Journal within weeks. News crossed the Atlantic quickly, and British surgeons began using ether before the end of 1846. The episode shows a recurring pattern in innovation: an invention becomes consequential when evidence, distribution and professional networks let strangers reproduce it.

5 · Success exposed a harder problem

Making a patient unconscious was only the beginning. Ether and chloroform could depress breathing, cause vomiting, ignite near flame or deliver an unpredictable dose. As surgery grew longer and more ambitious, someone had to manage the airway, circulation and depth of anaesthesia continuously. That need gradually separated anaesthesia from the surgeon's side task into its own skilled discipline.

6 · Pain-free surgery changed surgery itself

Anaesthesia did not by itself solve infection; operations could be painless and still deadly before antisepsis. But once pain and movement were controlled, surgeons could work more deliberately and enter body cavities that speed alone had made nearly impossible. Anaesthesia and antisepsis then reinforced each other, expanding the feasible frontier of surgery.

7 · The lasting invention was a safety system

By the twentieth century, machines delivered measured gases, endotracheal tubes protected airways, monitoring made hidden deterioration visible and trained anaesthetists managed the whole physiological state. Ether Day is remembered because it changed what the public believed possible. Its deeper legacy is that a dramatic demonstration eventually became a quiet, repeatable system whose success is measured by how little the patient notices.
