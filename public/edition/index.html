The lead

The AI capital race has reached the public market before its economics have settled.

Revenue growth is exceptional. So are the funding need, governance design and uncertainty hidden by the headline loss.
01 · AI, capital markets & governanceAssociated Press / regulatory filing · Monday

Anthropic filed for a Nasdaq listing after generating more than $16 billion of first-half revenue—and an $80.7 billion net loss dominated by a financing charge.

What changed

The Claude developer filed to trade as ANT next month, disclosing fourteenfold year-on-year revenue growth, continued unprofitability, 50.1% founder voting control and a safety trust with director-appointment powers.

Why it matters

The filing turns the AI financing debate into an investable security: public shareholders may fund infrastructure at historic scale while founders and a separate trust retain unusual control over commercial and safety decisions.

Watch next

The final valuation, proceeds, free float, cash operating loss after the $72.4 billion accounting charge, use of funds and the actual powers of the Long-Term Benefit Trust. The filing is public; reports of a $100 billion raise and a $1.5–2 trillion valuation are estimates, not settled terms.

A record candidate is also a test of what public ownership means when control stays concentrated

Anthropic's 335-page filing says first-half revenue exceeded $16 billion, fourteen times the year-earlier level. It also reports an $80.7 billion net loss, including a one-time $72.4 billion accounting charge tied to financing agreements. That distinction matters: the headline loss is not a clean measure of cash burn, but the company still says it is unprofitable and needs enormous infrastructure investment. The filing proposes a Nasdaq listing under ANT and says proceeds could fund acquisitions, technology and expansion. Outside estimates put the raise as high as $100 billion and valuation between $1.5 trillion and $2 trillion, but neither figure has been priced. Governance is equally important. Seven founders would hold 50.1% of votes through a special class, while a Long-Term Benefit Trust can appoint directors to protect public safety. Investors are therefore being offered economic exposure without ordinary proportional control, alongside a safety mechanism whose independence will be tested only when safety and revenue genuinely conflict.

Technology & work

AI spending is becoming a workforce-design decision, not only a technology budget.

The revealing number will be how many people accept—and what work Microsoft rebuilds after they leave.
02 · Enterprise AI, work & capital allocationMicrosoft / Associated Press · Monday

Microsoft offered voluntary buyouts to 60,000 employees—about 27% of its global workforce—as AI infrastructure spending accelerates.

What changed

Eligible employees in Canada, India, the United Kingdom and the United States can take a new voluntary exit offer; Microsoft had about 223,000 staff in June and reports quarterly results on Wednesday.

Why it matters

This is not evidence that AI has already replaced 60,000 jobs. It is a large-scale invitation to reshape the workforce while capital moves into data centres, chips and the work Microsoft says matters most.

Watch next

Take-up, eligibility and severance terms, whether involuntary cuts follow, where headcount changes concentrate, and Wednesday's capital-expenditure and productivity evidence. The offer is confirmed; the causal share attributable to AI rather than wider cost discipline is not quantified.

A voluntary programme reduces friction, but it does not answer what the organisation will look like afterward

Microsoft's chief people officer described the programme as a new Voluntary Exit Offer available to eligible employees in four major labour markets. The 60,000-person scope is more than a quarter of the global workforce, although an offer is not a completed departure. The company had already announced 4,800 job cuts in July and reportedly considered U.S. buyouts for about 8,750 people in May. At the same time, industry data-centre investment is expected to exceed $700 billion this year, raising the cost of chips, memory and power. That creates a visible allocation choice: spend more on compute while reducing or redirecting labour. The honest interpretation is narrower than 'AI took the jobs.' Microsoft has not published how many roles it expects to eliminate, how many volunteers it wants or how savings map to AI investment. But it has made organisational participation optional at a scale large enough to change skills, institutional memory and delivery risk.

You already know

The offer covers 60,000 eligible employees in four countries and follows earlier reductions this year.

New now

Microsoft is testing voluntary attrition as a global restructuring instrument while its AI capital programme expands.

Confirmed scope

60,000 employees can receive the offer; that is roughly 27% of a 223,000-person workforce.

Still unknown

Microsoft has not disclosed expected acceptance, role mix, savings or whether the offer prevents later layoffs.

Qatar & capital

Qatar is using an anchor mandate to try to build a market around the money.

The quality test is not only returns; it is whether the programme leaves behind deeper local research, trading and management capability.
04 · Qatar, capital markets & asset managementQatar News Agency · Monday

Doha Investment increased Qatar's Active Asset Management Initiative to QAR 5 billion from QAR 2.6 billion.

What changed

The 2024 initiative has deployed QAR 2.6 billion through Ashmore, Fiera Capital, Franklin Templeton and Lesha Bank; the larger mandate is intended to add managers and deepen Qatar's public-market ecosystem.

Why it matters

The policy is trying to create more than a temporary buying programme: external managers receive investable scale, while Qatar seeks analyst coverage, liquidity, competition and a larger domestic asset-management industry.

Watch next

New manager appointments, mandate sizes, benchmark and risk rules, domestic versus regional allocation, fees, measured liquidity effects and whether private local managers gain capability. QAR 5 billion is the expanded initiative size; it is not evidence that the additional QAR 2.4 billion has already been deployed.

An anchor allocation can change market plumbing—but only if the mandate creates durable participants

The initiative began under the Qatar Investment Authority in 2024 and has so far placed QAR 2.6 billion with four local and international managers. Doha Investment has now lifted the programme to QAR 5 billion and says it will broaden participation. An anchor mandate can solve a coordination problem: managers may not build local teams, research or products until assets are available, while capital owners may hesitate to allocate until that ecosystem exists. Committed capital can move both sides at once. But a larger pool does not automatically create a healthier market. The programme's value depends on mandate duration, performance discipline, fee economics, trading behaviour and whether knowledge remains in Qatar when a contract ends. Public information does not yet show the size or terms of each mandate, how the additional capital will be staged or what ecosystem outcomes will be measured beyond investment performance.

You already know

The initiative is increasing to QAR 5 billion and currently works with four named asset managers.

New now

Qatar is nearly doubling the programme after the first QAR 2.6 billion of deployment, signalling continuation rather than a one-off pilot.

Deployed base

QAR 2.6 billion has been placed through four managers since the 2024 launch.

Execution gap

The announcement does not yet disclose new managers, mandate terms, deployment dates or ecosystem targets.

Curiosity Corner

A 74-centimetre armoured amphibian appears to have lived its entire life in a salty Triassic sea—something no living amphibian can do.

Two nearly complete 237-million-year-old skeletons of Gerrothorax maritimus were preserved far from the ancient shoreline, and strontium isotopes in the bones match seawater from the period. The researchers propose that bony armour limited the skin-based water and salt exchange that makes modern amphibians poor ocean residents, shifting regulation toward gills and gut. The fossils strongly support a marine life; soft tissue is gone, so the exact salt-control mechanism remains an inference.

The Long View · Daily history

6 min

Dutch Republic & maritime Asia · 1594–1799

LV

The modern share began as an answer to a voyage too long, expensive and dangerous for one merchant to finance—and became a claim on an empire.

The VOC pooled public subscriptions, recorded ownership in ledgers and allowed those claims to be sold without recalling the ships. That separation between an enduring enterprise and a liquid investor helped create a securities market. It also financed monopoly, coercion and colonial violence on a scale private voyages could not reach.

Why remember this?

Transferable ownership solved a real capital problem: investors could exit without forcing the enterprise to liquidate. The same abstraction also widened the distance between owners and the human consequences of what their capital funded. Liquidity is an institutional achievement, not a moral verdict on the activity it makes scalable.
1 · A single voyage tied up capital until ships returned—or disappeared

The first Dutch ventures to Asia were expedition companies. Merchants pooled money for a particular fleet, bought ships and cargo, waited years for the round trip and then divided whatever survived. The first Amsterdam fleet left in 1595 with four ships and 249 people; two ships and 87 people returned in 1597. Its cargo only roughly covered the cost, but it proved the route. More companies followed, competing for crews, supplies and Asian trade. Each expedition reset the financing problem. Long distance, warfare, disease and uncertain prices made the venture too capital-hungry and slow for ordinary partnership finance.

2 · The 1602 charter pooled rivals and opened the subscription book

The States General merged six regional trading groups into the Verenigde Oostindische Compagnie, or VOC, and granted it a monopoly east of the Cape of Good Hope. From August 1602, residents of the Dutch Republic could subscribe in an amount of their choosing. Amsterdam's archive counts 1,143 subscribers there, including 466 investors who committed no more than 1,000 guilders: bakers, carpenters, surgeons, lawyers and household servants as well as rich merchants. Capital was recorded in a shareholder register rather than represented by a standard-value paper certificate. The important innovation was not a pretty document. It was a legally recognised claim on a continuing pool of enterprise assets.

3 · Transferability let the investor leave while the ships stayed at work

VOC shares could be sold, exchanged or pledged as collateral. Transfers were entered by a company bookkeeper and witnessed by directors. That meant an investor who needed cash did not have to demand that the company sell a ship or wind up a voyage. Another investor could buy the claim. A surviving receipt bought by Amsterdam grocer Theunis Jansz in 1606 passed through several owners before it was sold from an orphan's estate in 1739. The object endured because ownership had become separable from the original subscriber. Liquidity did not remove the commercial risk; it made the risk transferable.

4 · A market grew around one company's uncertain future

The company initially expected to account after ten years, but its capital became effectively long-lived. By 1611 Amsterdam had a purpose-built bourse, supported by the Exchange Bank established in 1609, where merchants could meet, settle payments and trade. VOC prospects were uncertain enough to sustain both investment and speculation: returns depended on war, weather, Asian prices and ships that could take years to come home. Share trading, forward contracts, options and short selling developed around those unknowns. The stock market was not designed in one act. It accumulated from registers, notaries, settlement institutions, gathering places and repeated disputes.

5 · Even the dividend showed how physical the enterprise remained

Modern shares feel abstract because distributions arrive as numbers in an account. Early VOC dividends could arrive as the cargo itself. Amsterdam's archive records Agneta Cock receiving portions of her entitlement in pepper, mace and nutmeg. The company later paid cash, commodities, bonds or nothing, depending on directors and circumstances. That gap between a tradeable claim in Amsterdam and warehouses full of spice, timber and textiles reveals the financial machine clearly: the share converted uncertain material flows across oceans into an ownership account that could circulate at home before the goods did.

6 · The financial breakthrough cannot be separated from the coercive charter

The VOC was not merely a clever logistics company. Its charter authorised treaty-making, fortification, force and administration of justice. It used those powers to seize territory, enforce spice monopolies and subject people across the Indonesian archipelago to violence and exploitation. Public subscription and liquid trading did not cause colonialism, but they gave the enterprise a larger, more persistent capital base and allowed distant owners to trade claims without confronting each operation directly. The institutional lesson therefore has two halves: permanent, transferable capital can organise risk and build capacity across generations; it can also scale domination while making responsibility feel remote.
