01 · Qatar, energy & global economyInternational Energy Agency · Thursday

The IEA says the Hormuz shock can permanently reroute energy—without making the Middle East replaceable.

What changed

The World Energy Outlook 2026 estimates that the disruption has removed a cumulative 2.8 billion barrels of oil and 60 billion cubic metres of gas from Gulf supply, then models a prolonged-risk case in which oil transit through Hormuz falls from 20 million barrels a day before the crisis to 8.5 million in 2030 and below 2 million in 2035.

Why it matters

This is a direct strategic issue for Qatar: oil can gain expensive bypass capacity, but the IEA says there is no feasible alternative route for Gulf LNG, while importers are accelerating diversification, electrification, solar, nuclear and efficiency to reduce exposure.

Watch next

The duration of the Hormuz disruption, final approvals for roughly 7 million barrels a day of proposed bypass pipelines, LNG contract behaviour, inventory rebuilding and whether importers convert emergency measures into lasting demand reduction.

The most important finding is not that Hormuz becomes irrelevant, but that the cost of depending on it becomes investable

The IEA describes the 2026 disruption as the largest energy-supply shock on record. In its prolonged-risk case, about 7 million barrels a day of bypass-pipeline capacity is built or considered at an estimated cost of $40 billion, adding roughly $2 to each Middle Eastern export barrel using those routes. Importers also substitute supply and reduce demand. Together those changes could cut oil transit sharply, yet the Middle East remains the world’s largest oil exporter. LNG is more exposed: the report identifies no feasible bypass from the Gulf, leaving alternative global supply and customer demand response as the main buffers. The crisis is also accelerating electricity use, solar, batteries and electric vehicles. Cooling demand has overtaken heating demand for the first time, and electricity approaches 30% of final energy consumption by 2035 across the report’s exploratory scenarios.

02 · AI, capital markets & business modelsAnthropic, SEC and Reuters · Thursday

Anthropic raised about $81.3 billion in the largest IPO on record—and public markets now have to price a frontier AI lab in real time.

What changed

Anthropic sold 980 million Class A shares at $83, valuing the company at about $1.5 trillion; the stock opened at $111.50 and closed at $97.17, 17% above the offer price and roughly $1.77 trillion on a fully diluted basis.

Why it matters

The flotation gives one frontier-model developer exceptional funding capacity and creates a public benchmark for the economics, governance and risk of AI labs that were previously valued mainly in private rounds.

Watch next

Anthropic’s first quarterly disclosures, cash use and compute commitments, revenue concentration, the quality of recurring enterprise demand, governance under the public-benefit structure and what happens when lock-ups expire.

The IPO creates both a financing event and a new disclosure regime for frontier AI

Anthropic priced above its marketed range and increased the offering to 980 million shares. The company itself sold 956.35 million of them, while existing holders sold 23.65 million; the company receives no proceeds from the secondary portion. Underwriters also have a 30-day option for up to 147 million additional shares. The record raise matters because model training, inference capacity, data centres and talent require capital at a scale that few companies can fund internally. Public ownership also changes the information environment: quarterly revenue, expense, customer and cash-flow evidence will now arrive on a fixed timetable. The closing valuation shows extraordinary confidence in future AI demand, but the retreat from the $111.50 opening also shows that scarcity and excitement can create a wide first-day range before operating evidence catches up.

You already know

The offer size, price, share allocation and SEC prospectus are filed; the stock closed its first session at $97.17.

New now

A frontier-model developer now has a liquid public valuation and a record pool of fresh equity capital rather than only private-market marks.

Capital raised

980 million shares at $83 produced about $81.3 billion of gross proceeds, mostly from newly issued shares.

Public-market verdict

The shares opened 34% above the offer but closed 17% higher, leaving a very large valuation with a wide first-day range.

03 · Consumer behaviour, retail & operating modelsWalmart and LSEG · Thursday

Walmart’s quarter shows a retailer using delivery, advertising and membership to fund a stronger value proposition.

What changed

Revenue rose 5.2%, Walmart US ecommerce grew 23%, Walmart Connect advertising grew 36% excluding acquired businesses, global membership-fee revenue rose 17%, and US marketplace and fulfilment-services net sales increased 49%.

Why it matters

The useful business lesson is the reinforcement between low prices and higher-margin services: traffic creates advertising inventory, marketplace selection and membership value, while those revenues create more room to invest in price and fulfilment.

Watch next

Holiday traffic and average ticket, gross margin after price investment, delivery economics, marketplace quality, advertising growth and whether higher-income customers continue shifting spend toward value retailers.

Walmart is turning the same customer visit into several different economic relationships

US comparable sales increased 2.9% excluding fuel, while digital and service businesses grew much faster. Ecommerce creates convenience and more frequent contact; the marketplace widens assortment without owning every unit of inventory; fulfilment can be sold to third-party merchants; advertising monetises purchase intent; and membership collects recurring revenue while making customers more likely to return. LSEG notes that traffic and average ticket both increased and that Walmart gained share across income groups, including households earning more than $100,000. Walmart says it now has about 7,700 US rollbacks and is using tariff refunds to support price investment. The model is powerful only if fulfilment quality, seller standards and advertising load preserve trust rather than extracting it.

You already know

Revenue, ecommerce, advertising, marketplace and membership all grew in the reported quarter, with US comparable sales up 2.9% excluding fuel.

New now

The service layers are becoming large enough to reinforce the core price-and-convenience offer rather than sit beside it as experiments.

Core retail

US comparable sales rose 2.9% excluding fuel, supported by both traffic and average ticket.

Service layers

US ecommerce rose 23%, advertising 36%, and marketplace plus fulfilment-services net sales 49%.

04 · AI infrastructure, trade & regulationUS Commerce Department · Thursday

US chip controls now follow advanced computing into overseas data centres, not only across a physical border.

What changed

A new interim rule effective 19 November requires licences when advanced US-origin computing is supplied to overseas data centres that provide significant remote capacity to entities headquartered in Macau or US arms-embargo countries, and adds licensing for advanced-chip shipments to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, subject to defined exceptions.

Why it matters

Cloud access had become a route around physical export controls; the rule shifts compliance toward customer ownership, end use and compute delivery, affecting data-centre operators, chip exporters and AI companies well beyond China itself.

Watch next

Licence decisions, how ‘significant volume’ is applied, the 60-day comment process, enforcement against indirect ownership, responses from affected governments and whether Chinese labs materially slow or accelerate substitution toward domestic chips.

Curiosity Corner

Bogong moths navigate 1,000 kilometres with a compass that needs violet-blue light.

Researchers flew migratory moths in a non-magnetic laboratory under a randomized star field. Rotating the Earth-strength magnetic field by 180 degrees reversed their heading; reversing polarity alone did not, showing that the insects read the field’s angle of inclination rather than magnetic north and south. Remove violet-blue light below 500 nanometres, or add weak broadband radio-frequency noise, and the magnetic orientation disappears—evidence consistent with a light-driven radical-pair mechanism, possibly involving cryptochrome. The moths already use the stars, so the discovery is a second independent compass: a tiny brain combines celestial and quantum-sensitive cues to reach alpine caves it has never visited.

The Long View · Daily history

1973–2026

06

The oil shock of 1973 created a treaty-backed insurance system for energy importers

The International Energy Agency was not built mainly as a forecasting shop. It began as a legally binding answer to a coordination problem: how to stop importing countries from competing against one another when oil disappears.

Why remember this?

1 · The 1973–74 crisis exposed a political weakness as much as a physical shortage

The embargo and production cuts pushed prices sharply higher and revealed how dependent industrial economies had become on imported oil. Each government faced a strong temptation to secure its own supply, restrict exports or strike a separate arrangement. That reaction could deepen a shortage for everyone else. The problem was therefore not only how many barrels existed, but whether importing states could share information, restrain demand and accept rules that still bound them when domestic pressure was highest. A voluntary promise made in calm conditions was unlikely to survive a severe crisis.

2 · Importers answered with a treaty, not merely another committee

A Washington energy conference in February 1974 created the negotiating machinery. On 18 November, sixteen countries signed the Agreement on an International Energy Program and placed the new autonomous International Energy Agency within the OECD in Paris. The legal form was deliberate. The founders had watched countries pull apart during the previous crisis and wanted emergency sharing to remain binding even when compliance became costly. The agreement combined oil-security obligations with conservation, long-term reduction of import dependence and a common information system.

3 · The system stored time before it stored policy ambition

Members first committed to reserves sufficient for 60 days without net imports, later rising to the familiar 90-day obligation. The emergency design also included demand restraint, fuel switching and allocation of available supply. A shortfall of 7% could trigger the sharing machinery for the group or an individual member. Companies would provide current supply data so the agency could calculate needs and obligations. The reserves were not meant to replace production indefinitely. They bought governments weeks or months in which to avoid panic, organise logistics and adjust consumption.

4 · A rigid trigger proved less useful than a flexible collective signal

The disruptions around the Iranian Revolution did not cleanly activate the treaty threshold, despite severe economic damage. That experience pushed the agency toward more flexible use of stocks and demand restraint. Its first collective action came before the 1991 Gulf War. It acted again after Hurricanes Katrina and Rita in 2005, during Libya’s supply disruption in 2011 and twice after Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022. Each release was partly physical supply and partly communication: it told markets that members would draw a shared buffer rather than hoard it.

5 · The sixth action in 2026 was the largest—and showed both the system’s reach and its limit

In March 2026 members made 400 million barrels available in response to Middle Eastern disruption. The mechanism had grown from a club protecting a few importers into a stabiliser operating alongside non-member consumers, producers and OPEC. Yet the IEA still describes releases as short-term responses to severe supply loss, not a device for choosing a preferred price or replacing long-term supply. Its deeper legacy is institutional: stock obligations, data, demand plans and agreed decision rules exist before the emergency begins. That does not remove geopolitical risk. It changes the first response from improvisation to a rehearsed collective option.
