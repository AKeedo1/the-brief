Iran is accelerating attacks on commercial shipping and widening the operating area beyond Hormuz, including waters off Qatar and the UAE.

What changed

Two senior Iranian officials told Reuters that Tehran adopted new tactics roughly ten days ago to raise the pace and geographic reach of attacks on vessels it considers in violation of its restrictions. Maritime-security reporting recorded at least five incidents around Hormuz in 48 hours, roughly matching the previous peak on 3–5 October. A U.S. military official said Iran is using unarmed spotter boats after losing much of its radar capability. Security analysis places recent targeting outside the strait, including waters off Qatar and the UAE.

Why it matters

The risk has shifted from a single chokepoint to the approaches around it. That raises the cost of moving Qatari LNG and regional oil even when a ship can technically transit Hormuz, because insurers, crews and naval escorts must price a larger threat area. Iranian officials explicitly linked the strategy to keeping oil prices high before the U.S. midterms, but that claimed political objective cannot be independently verified.

A wider attack box changes economics before it changes physical flow

Shipping risk is priced across routes, not only at the moment a vessel crosses the strait. If surveillance and attacks extend toward Qatar and the UAE, vessels spend longer inside the high-risk zone and escorts must cover more water. Insurers can raise premiums, restrict cover or demand security conditions even when cargo volumes continue. That helps explain why delivered energy costs can remain high despite visible transits.

Qatar

The state has moved from describing resilience to publishing its fiscal cost.

The revenue and expenditure figures are official first-half aggregates. They do not reveal the monthly path, the exact contribution of Ras Laffan damage or the remaining fiscal-buffer capacity.
02 · Qatar policy, public finance & resilienceAmiri Diwan · Tuesday

Qatar disclosed a 42% fall in first-half revenue and a 14% fall in spending while keeping essential services and development priorities intact.

What changed

Opening the Shura Council’s annual session, the Amir said the closure of Hormuz and damage to energy facilities at Ras Laffan disrupted a significant share of exports. Total state revenue fell 42% in the first half of 2026 and public expenditure declined 14%. He said essential services and development priorities were preserved, supported by reserves, lower public debt, conservative oil-price assumptions and non-oil revenue developed before the crisis.

Why it matters

This is the clearest official quantification yet of how the regional war has reached Qatar’s public finances. The disclosure also defines the policy response: fiscal discipline and spending efficiency without abandoning development. That protects momentum in the short term, but a prolonged export constraint would test how long buffers can substitute for hydrocarbon cash flow.

Watch next

The next budget update; monthly LNG export recovery; sovereign-asset or debt issuance; project reprioritisation; new private-sector and investment legislation requested from the Shura Council; whether essential services remain insulated; and whether revenue normalises faster than spending.

Buffers buy time, but composition decides endurance

A country can absorb a temporary export shock through accumulated reserves, conservative budgeting and borrowing capacity. Cutting expenditure by much less than revenue supports households and the investment pipeline, which can prevent the shock from multiplying through the domestic economy. The trade-off is that the budget absorbs the difference. If exports recover, that bridge works. If disruption persists, spending quality and prioritisation become more important than an across-the-board cut.

Regional escalation

Saudi Arabia and the Houthis moved into reciprocal attacks on energy infrastructure as the coalition opened a new operation in Yemen.

What changed

The Saudi-led coalition announced an operation to neutralise Houthi capabilities threatening Saudi Arabia, Yemen and Red Sea shipping, then struck energy terminals and fuel reserves in Yemen. The coalition said it intercepted ballistic missiles toward Haradh and Dhahran; debris fell in a Dhahran residential area. The coalition says it took precautions to limit civilian harm, but the independent damage picture inside Yemen remains incomplete.

Target expansion makes restoration harder than interception

Missile defence can reduce immediate damage, but commercial restoration depends on predictable risk. Airports, refineries, offices, ports and storage sites each bring different safety inspections, insurance conditions and workforce exposure. Once both sides identify energy systems as legitimate targets, the region faces not only lost output but longer operating uncertainty around facilities that remain intact.

Russia struck Kyiv twice in roughly a day, hitting homes and energy facilities while Ukraine said allied warning failed again.

What changed

Five Russian ballistic missiles hit Kyiv on Tuesday, killing two people in a high-rise building, injuring 21 and striking a thermal power plant, according to Ukrainian officials. Intelligence sharing is a chain, not a switch

Early warning requires detection, classification, communication and delivery to the right defence and civil-alert systems before impact. A government can continue sharing intelligence in general while a specific warning arrives too late, lacks confidence or never reaches operators. That is why the public statements do not necessarily contradict each other, but they do signal a serious reliability problem.

Global economy

China’s September exports surged 36.3% and pushed its nine-month trade surplus to about $938 billion.

What changed

China’s customs agency said exports rose 36.3% from a year earlier in September, far above expectations, while imports also accelerated. The trade surplus for January through September reached roughly $938 billion. Passenger-car exports rose 64% in September and exceeded 7.1 million vehicles in the first nine months, as manufacturers used foreign markets to offset weaker demand at home.

Why it matters

The strength is economically supportive for China but politically destabilising abroad. A surplus of this scale increases pressure from Europe and other trading partners for quotas, tariffs or currency adjustment, especially when Chinese industrial capacity is meeting soft domestic consumption. Faster imports show demand is not absent, but the imbalance remains large.

A trade surplus is both demand and displacement

Exports add to domestic production and employment, while imports reveal internal demand for foreign inputs and goods. But when export growth greatly exceeds import growth, the difference appears as a surplus and as competitive pressure in destination markets. Cars make the mechanism visible: Chinese factories gain scale abroad while local manufacturers and governments respond with quotas or tariffs.

Apple’s proposed answer combines face, voice, recent touch and the nearby iPhone. The technical design matters, but the quality test is whether households can predict its decisions.

AI security

The warning is about a new payload for an old botnet problem.

Anthropic demonstrated components in a controlled test network. It did not observe an AI system independently escaping and replicating across the public internet.
07 · AI safety, cybersecurity & connected devicesAnthropic / Reuters · Tuesday

Anthropic says small AI models may be months from replicating across ordinary connected devices without relying on a data centre.

What changed

Anthropic reported experiments in which a malicious agent distributed small models across a simplified network of commercial internet-connected hardware. A nine-billion-parameter model ran on eight of nine device types tested. The company described a scenario in which an agent could build a botnet, resist takedown and use compromised devices to improve its capacity. Outside experts told Reuters the concern is plausible, while one called the timeline vendor exaggeration.

Why it matters

Traditional botnets execute malware; a model-bearing botnet could also interpret environments and adapt its behaviour. The immediate significance is defensive, not science-fictional: device makers, cloud providers and network operators may need controls that detect model deployment and coordinated inference, not only known malicious files. The result remains a lab demonstration and does not show an autonomous system successfully spreading on the open internet.

Watch next

Publication of methods and benchmarks; independent replication; minimum hardware and bandwidth requirements; safeguards in consumer devices; network signatures; cloud-provider response; whether capability improves in open models; and how policymakers distinguish credible risk from company advocacy.

Distribution can substitute for one powerful machine

A small model that is too weak on one device could divide storage or computation across many compromised devices and use a larger remote model for planning. Internet-of-things hardware is attractive because it is numerous, inconsistently patched and rarely monitored like a server. The defence therefore overlaps with ordinary cybersecurity: secure updates, segmented networks, device identity, rate limits and rapid revocation.

The Long View · Daily history

7 min

CHINA
10th–14th CENTURIES

047

Paper money was not invented by declaring paper valuable. It emerged because coins had become too heavy for a commercial economy.

Merchants in Sichuan turned deposit receipts into exchange; the Song state standardised them; later governments learned that printing could solve a shortage of money and create a crisis of trust. The first paper-currency system was both an administrative triumph and an early lesson in monetary limits.

Why remember this?

The familiar story says China invented paper money because it had paper and printing. Those technologies were necessary, but they were not the cause. The cause was a mismatch between a fast-growing economy and the physical currency available to serve it. Heavy iron coins, distant markets and expanding trade made private receipts useful. Government authority then made them scalable. But the same authority that guaranteed redemption also gained the power to issue more notes than the system could support. Paper money’s history is therefore not a simple march from metal to modernity. It is a history of logistics becoming trust, and trust becoming a public institution that had to be governed.
1 · Sichuan had a weight problem

Around the end of the tenth century, Sichuan’s trade relied heavily on iron coins. They were useful for small exchange but punishing for large transactions: strings of coins could weigh more than the goods being bought. Merchants began leaving coins with deposit shops and carrying paper receipts instead. The receipt represented a claim on stored value. It could be redeemed, but its real advantage was that people began accepting it directly, saving the next holder the journey back to the original coins.

2 · Private convenience created a public problem

The earliest jiaozi were issued by merchant houses, not invented in a palace. Their usefulness depended on confidence that the issuer held reserves and would redeem the note. Some houses overissued or failed, producing losses and demands for regulation. In the early eleventh century the Northern Song government restricted private issuance and established an official office to manage the currency. A commercial workaround had become a state monetary system.

3 · The first design was disciplined by expiry

Official jiaozi were not intended to circulate forever. The government issued them in batches, commonly with a three-year life, after which holders exchanged old notes for new ones. The notes were initially linked to reserves of iron coin, and issue limits were meant to protect convertibility. Expiry helped the state count and replace notes, made counterfeiting harder and reminded users that paper’s value came from an organised redemption system rather than from the material itself.

4 · Printing mattered, but administration mattered more

China already possessed sophisticated papermaking and woodblock printing. Notes could carry seals, complex patterns, denominations and warnings against forgery. Yet a technically impressive note was worthless without offices that issued it, taxes that accepted it, courts that punished fraud and merchants willing to price goods in it. The monetary invention was institutional: a standard claim that strangers across a region could recognise and transfer.

5 · War turned flexibility into temptation

Paper allowed governments to support commerce without finding, minting and moving an equal weight of metal. It also made emergency finance easier. Under the Southern Song, prolonged military pressure and fiscal strain encouraged larger note issues. When issuance outran the goods, taxes and reserves supporting confidence, prices rose and notes traded below their stated value. The failure was not that paper lacked intrinsic metal. It was that the promise embedded in each note became less credible as more promises were printed.

6 · The Yuan tried to make paper universal

After conquering China, the Mongol Yuan dynasty built on earlier experiments and used paper currency across a much larger territory. Early Yuan notes were connected to silver values, helping unify markets that had used different regional monies. Marco Polo later described the astonishing spectacle of subjects accepting paper under imperial authority. For a time the system supported domestic and long-distance trade. But warfare, court expenditure and repeated issuance weakened the silver link and drove severe depreciation late in the dynasty.

7 · Counterfeiting revealed what a banknote really was

Governments used special paper, multiple colours, seals and elaborate designs, and threatened counterfeiters with death. A surviving Ming note in the Bank of England Museum offers both a reward for informants and a warning of execution. The severity makes sense: a counterfeit was not merely a fake object. It was an unauthorised claim on the trust, tax capacity and purchasing power of the state. Security features protected the political promise as much as the printed sheet.

8 · Paper money did not permanently replace coins

China’s early currency systems remained plural. Copper and iron coins, silver, commodities and several forms of paper circulated in different regions and periods. When confidence in official notes collapsed, people returned to metal or priced paper at a discount. This is why describing jiaozi as the first modern fiat money can mislead. Early notes often began as redeemable claims and operated alongside other monies; their meaning changed as policy and circumstances changed.

9 · The lasting invention was portable trust

A coin carries part of its story in its material. A banknote asks the holder to trust an issuer, a rulebook and a community of other users. Song merchants discovered that this abstraction could free trade from the weight of metal. Governments discovered that they could standardise and expand it. The subsequent failures did not erase the invention. They clarified its operating condition: money can become lighter than the value it moves only when the institutions behind it remain heavier than doubt.
