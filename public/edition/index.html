What changed

02 · Climate disaster & infrastructureNepal authorities, scientists + AP · Tuesday night

Nepal began mass burials as the flood toll passed 1,000 and the adaptation limit came into view.

What changed

The confirmed toll across Nepal and China passed 1,000, with Nepal reporting 987 deaths and 3,916 people still missing in the latest accessible count. Search teams continued while Kathmandu buried dozens of victims. Scientists said the 26 August event began with a glacier collapse powerful enough to register as a magnitude 5.2 seismic event, sending water, rock and mud through valleys exposed by rapid Himalayan warming.

Why it matters

Yesterday established the hydropower and rescue failure. Today’s evidence clarifies the hazard: this was a compound mountain event capable of overwhelming defences designed around older conditions. Nearly two billion people depend on rivers that begin in the Himalayas. The choice is no longer simply stronger barriers; it includes where settlements and power projects should exist, how workers receive warnings, and when retreat is safer than rebuilding.

Warming changes the map, not only the weather

The Himalayas combine steep gradients, earthquakes, glaciers, unstable moraine and densely used river corridors. Warming can enlarge glacial lakes, weaken frozen ground and alter rainfall; a collapse can then entrain rock and sediment into a moving wall. The event damaged villages and a significant share of Nepal’s hydropower system at once. Traditional adaptation assumes a known hazard range that stronger construction and evacuation can manage. Scientists interviewed by AP said some areas may be crossing a threshold where those measures are insufficient, changing the burden of proof before rebuilding.

Global economy

Broad agreement produced a record of disagreement, not a common policy.

04 · G20, China & global tradeU.S. Treasury + AP · Tuesday night

China withheld consent from the G20 finance statement as 19 members challenged export-led imbalances.

What changed

The U.S. presidency issued a chair’s statement rather than a consensus communiqué after China objected to four paragraphs on global imbalances, trade, financial regulation and growth. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the other 19 members present agreed that a continuing stream of cheap exports from non-market economies was unsustainable. China, whose goods trade surplus reached nearly $1.2 trillion in 2025, rejected the framing.

Why it matters

The 19–1 split is politically striking but operationally incomplete. The United States and Europe increasingly agree that Chinese overcapacity creates domestic pressure, yet do not share one remedy. A chair’s statement can isolate a dissenter; it cannot create enforcement. Consequences will come through national trade measures, procurement rules and negotiations with Beijing.

A surplus is a fact; overcapacity is a diagnosis

China’s export strength reflects scale, productivity, subsidies, weak consumption and industrial strategy in proportions that vary by sector. Importers see low prices but also plant closures and dependence. The G20 text says persistent imbalances create vulnerabilities and calls for adjustment by surplus and deficit economies, yet China objected. When the largest surplus economy rejects the diagnosis, action migrates from the forum to tariffs, subsidies and bilateral bargaining.

This matters more than a weak equity session. Sovereign yields price mortgages, corporate debt, projects and assets. Oil creates an inflation impulse just as governments ask investors to absorb record borrowing. If central banks cannot ease, refinancing costs move through public budgets and private investment. For a long-horizon portfolio, the signal is a higher discount rate and wider outcomes—not a one-day reason to trade.

Why bonds can matter more than the equity headline

A bond yield combines expected short rates, inflation and a term premium. Oil can lift inflation and make central banks less willing to cut. Deficits increase the debt markets must absorb. Political uncertainty can add a premium. When those arrive together, prices fall and yields rise. The impact spreads because corporate bonds, mortgages and infrastructure are priced from sovereign curves. The test is persistence: a brief energy spike can reverse, while a lasting rise in term premium changes capital allocation.

Technology & rules

AI governance is becoming a market-access strategy.

The principles were described by U.S. officials; signatories, legal effect and enforcement were incomplete at publication.
06 · AI, regulation & industrial powerG20 technology meeting + Reuters · Tuesday

The United States asked G20 members to treat most AI uses under existing law.

What changed

At the G20 innovation ministerial, U.S. technology adviser Michael Kratsios promoted 'Carolina Principles' under which governments would avoid entirely new AI regulation and write new rules only for genuinely novel situations. The approach aligns with U.S. AI companies and the effort to keep international markets inside the American technology ecosystem. Accessible reporting did not establish which countries signed or how binding the principles are.

Why it matters

The argument shifts the burden of proof. Governments would first test whether competition, consumer, labour, product-safety or cybersecurity law covers the harm. That can reduce duplication and speed deployment, but can leave gaps where autonomous systems create scale or opacity that old law did not anticipate. Regulatory compatibility can determine which models, clouds and chips become default infrastructure.

Watch next

The final signatory list and text; EU and Chinese responses; treatment of high-risk agents, children and employment decisions; liability for autonomous actions; export-control alignment; and exceptions after recent agent-security failures.

Existing law first is a framework, not no regulation

AI can mislead a consumer, discriminate in hiring, violate privacy or facilitate a cyberattack; each harm may already sit inside a regulator’s mandate. The U.S. proposal says novelty should trigger an AI-specific layer. The difficult question is whether novelty lies in the harm or in autonomy, speed and cross-domain action. American firms favour interoperability and lower friction; other jurisdictions favour duties before deployment because model behaviour is hard to inspect afterward.

The Long View · Daily history

7 min read

GLOBAL
1974–PRESENT

09

The Montreal Protocol worked because the treaty was designed to become stricter after it was signed.

The world did not wait for perfect certainty, demand identical sacrifice from every country or freeze one rulebook in 1987. It built a mechanism that could tighten as evidence improved, finance conversion in poorer states and let industry replace chemicals on a schedule. The ozone layer’s slow repair is the result of that institutional design.

Why remember this?

International cooperation survives uncertainty when evidence, adjustment, money and enforcement are built into the operating system—not left to goodwill after the ceremony.
1 · A useful chemical escaped the factory

Chlorofluorocarbons looked like an industrial triumph: stable, non-flammable and comparatively non-toxic, they served refrigeration, aerosols, foam and solvents. Stability was the hidden problem. CFCs persisted long enough to reach the stratosphere. In 1974, Mario Molina and F. Sherwood Rowland argued that ultraviolet light would break them apart and release chlorine atoms capable of destroying ozone repeatedly. The ozone layer absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation, so thinning meant higher risks to skin, eyes, crops and ecosystems. The mechanism was plausible and serious, but early observations did not yet offer the dramatic image that later organized public attention. Governments faced a familiar dilemma: constrain a valuable industry before every step of the damage was measured.

2 · The Antarctic hole turned a model into an alarm

In 1985, British Antarctic Survey scientists reported that springtime ozone over Antarctica had fallen far more than expected. The hole was not empty space but extreme seasonal thinning. Its scale surprised researchers because some automated datasets had filtered very low readings as errors. Fieldwork clarified why Antarctica was exceptional: cold polar clouds convert chlorine into reactive forms, and returning spring sunlight activates rapid destruction. The discovery did not replace the 1974 mechanism; it made the consequences visible. It also demonstrated the value of independent monitoring. Repeated atmospheric measurements created a common constraint governments and manufacturers had to explain.

3 · Vienna separated cooperation from sacrifice

The 1985 Vienna Convention created research, monitoring and information exchange without firm cuts. That sequencing solved a political problem. Countries could agree the atmosphere needed protection before agreeing which chemicals, quantities and dates to regulate. Scientific networks and diplomatic routines developed while evidence strengthened. When negotiators met in Montreal in 1987, they added controls to an existing framework. The first protocol required reductions in selected CFCs and halons but was designed for adjustment. Parties could tighten schedules as science, technology and economics changed. The agreement was a ratchet, not a once-and-for-all bargain.

4 · Different timetables made participation possible

A uniform immediate ban would have asked countries with different incomes and refrigeration needs to absorb the same cost. The protocol gave developing countries longer timetables and created a Multilateral Fund to pay agreed conversion costs, technical assistance and institutional support. This was part of compliance design, not charity. Rich countries had used most chemicals historically and possessed alternatives; poorer states needed refrigeration for food and medicine without obsolete equipment. Trade controls with non-parties also reduced the incentive for production to migrate. By 2009, Vienna and Montreal became the first treaties in the U.N. system to achieve universal participation.

5 · Industry changed when the schedule became credible

The protocol did not succeed because substitutes were perfect in advance. A credible phase-out changed the return to finding them. Manufacturers redesigned aerosols, foams, solvents and cooling equipment; governments tightened schedules at later meetings. Some replacements damaged ozone less but remained transitional. Hydrofluorocarbons protected ozone yet trapped heat, creating a climate problem. In 2016, parties adopted the Kigali Amendment to phase down HFCs through the same machinery. This is not one regulation picking one clean technology. It is policy creating demand for innovation, monitoring revealing consequences and the regime adjusting again.

6 · Recovery is slow enough to test patience

The 2022 scientific assessment concluded that nearly 99% of banned ozone-depleting substances had been phased out and recovery was advancing. Yet long atmospheric lifetimes mean Antarctic ozone is not expected to return to 1980 levels until roughly 2066, with earlier recovery elsewhere. Weather changes the hole year to year, and unexpected emissions can appear. Monitoring detected a rise in CFC-11 in the 2010s, allowing investigators to locate and reduce the source. The achievement is not that the problem was declared solved in 1987. Measurement, expert assessment, finance, trade rules, compliance review and amendment stayed active for decades.
